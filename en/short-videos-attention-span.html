Do short videos ruin your attention span? What the studies actually show

Research links heavy short video use with poorer attention, but it hasn't shown that short videos cause it. A 2025 review of 71 studies (98,299 people) found that people who watch more short videos tend to score lower on attention and self-control, and most of that evidence is correlation. A small 2023 experiment found that a TikTok break made people worse at remembering to do something they had planned, and a 2025 trial found that blocking mobile internet for two weeks improved sustained attention. So cutting back is a reasonable bet, not a proven cure.

What people mean by "my attention span is gone"

It usually sounds like this. You sit down to read 10 pages and pick up your phone after two. A 20-minute YouTube video feels long. You open Reels for a minute between tasks and forget what you were doing. The studies below test pieces of this: attention, self-control, and remembering what you meant to do.

The big review: a link, not proof

In their meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin, Nguyen and colleagues combined 71 studies covering 98,299 people. Heavier short video use was associated with poorer cognition overall (r = −0.34), and the strongest links were with attention (r = −0.38) and inhibitory control, the ability to hold back an impulse (r = −0.41). Heavier use also went with poorer mental health, most clearly stress and anxiety. The pattern held for young people and adults and across platforms. It was not linked to body image or self-esteem.

The authors describe these as correlates. A link this size could mean short videos wear down attention, or that people who already find it hard to focus, or who are stressed, watch more of them. Both can be true. Few of the studies looked at memory or reasoning.

An experiment with TikTok

Chiossi and colleagues (CHI 2023) asked 60 people to do a prospective memory task, which means remembering to carry out an action you planned earlier, with interruptions in between. During the breaks, separate groups used TikTok, Twitter, YouTube or nothing. Only the TikTok group's performance dropped. The authors put it down to the mix of short videos and quick switching from one to the next.

It is one small lab study, but it matches what many people notice: after a few minutes of Reels, it's harder to remember what you were about to do.

What happened when people blocked their phones

In a 2025 trial in PNAS Nexus, Castelo and colleagues asked 467 iPhone users to block all mobile internet (Wi-Fi and mobile data) with an app for two weeks. They could still call and text. Compared with the group waiting for their turn, they improved in sustained attention, mental health and subjective well-being. Daily screen time in the blocking group fell from 314 to 161 minutes, then went back up to 265 minutes after the two weeks.

Two cautions. Only 25.5% blocked on at least 10 of the 14 days, and most people who signed up already wanted to cut down. And because the study blocked the whole mobile internet, it can't say how much of the change came from short videos.

So should you cut back?

The evidence doesn't prove that short videos damage attention. It does show that heavy use and poorer attention tend to go together, that a TikTok break disrupted memory for plans in a lab, and that people who cut their phone use for two weeks focused better. If you feel your focus slipping, cutting back costs little and may help. If trouble concentrating is affecting school, work or daily life, talk to a doctor. That isn't something an app can fix.

Do short videos really reduce attention span?

They are linked, but cause and effect isn't proven. A 2025 review of 71 studies found that heavier short video use went with poorer attention and self-control. Most of the studies were correlational, so people who already find it hard to focus might simply watch more.

Is TikTok worse for attention than YouTube?

One 2023 lab study with 60 people found that a TikTok break hurt memory for planned actions while YouTube and Twitter breaks didn't. The 2025 review found similar links across short video platforms, including Reels and Shorts. There isn't enough evidence to rank the apps.

Can you get your attention span back?

In a 2025 trial, people who blocked mobile internet on their iPhones for two weeks did better on a sustained attention test. That study blocked all mobile internet, not only short videos, and most participants didn't block every day. It suggests cutting back can make a difference within weeks.

How much short video is too much?

No study has set a safe number of minutes. The research compares people who watch more with people who watch less. Your own Screen Time report is a better guide: if the time is more than you would choose, or you can't focus on things you want to do, that is a reason to cut back.
